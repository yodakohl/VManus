# Hundred-ninety-fifth pass: a second scribe writes the same instruction

## Second rendering

The second scribe was not told to make every card agree with the field frame.
For each card they used the registered surface most typical of that card's
field position in the 381 observed events. This changes 8/25 written surfaces:

- CH field: `chor chaiin cheky cheol chey` → `or daiin cheky ol dy`;
- D field: `dchol dchey daiin dal dar` → `dchol dchey daiin al dar`;
- O field: `or okain okeey ol olchedy` → `or okain qokeey ol olchedy`;
- Q field: `qokaiin qokal qokeey qoky qokedy` → `qokaiin okal qokeey qoky qokedy`;
- S field: `saiin shedal shecthy shcthy schedy` → `saiin shedal shecthy checthy schedy`.

## Readback

Despite the eight changes:

- all 25 surfaces are registered;
- all 25 uniquely recover the same exact cards and card values;
- all five field closures remain intact;
- four of five field modes are still recoverable by majority frame.

Only the open CH construction field loses visible mode harmony: four of its
five cards revert to their usual positional forms. This neatly matches the real
inventory, where CH-mode construction fields are sparse and often open, while
closed O/Q/S cells carry stronger concord.

## Workshop interpretation

Two scribes can therefore write the same source content in visibly different
ways without changing the learned card sequence. A careful master hand may
harmonize the whole field; a faster copyist may keep positional card habits and
signal the mode only partially. Both remain readable to someone who knows the
card inventory and the close conventions.

That gives the multi-scribe hypothesis a simple learning story:

1. memorize the card bodies and values;
2. learn closure and position;
3. optionally harmonize a field with CH/D/O/Q/S frames;
4. tolerate registered allographs when copying quickly around pictures.
