## A. Rational tuning paths with both closure and nonclosure

The prospective content is a whole tuning or interval argument. It names a
starting sound, successive intervals, alternative routes, the resulting sounds,
and assertions that endpoints agree or differ. The same interval operation must
act identically everywhere. Repeated multiplication and inversion compose ratios;
an endpoint comparison must agree with every path reaching that endpoint.
Instructional prose and proof clauses may vary in number under one grammar.

The complete owned numerical example is proposition IX of the *Sectio canonis*,
transmitted under Euclid's name, together with the complete second proof of VI
and propositions XII–XIV connecting ratios to musical intervals. The ancient
authorship and precise composition date are disputed. The source used is
[Charles-Émile Ruelle's French translation, digitized by Marc Szwajcer](https://remacle.org/bloodwolf/erudits/euclide/canon.htm).
Its [title image](https://remacle.org/bloodwolf/erudits/cleonide/harmon1.gif) was
actually viewed and explicitly names Ruelle. A
[library catalogue](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupname?key=Euclid)
dates the collected edition to 1884. No medieval manuscript image of this precise
passage was acquired. Ancient content, nineteenth-century translation and modern
digital presentation are distinct provenance layers.

**The other path and all relevant claims.** Proposition VI's second complete
proof takes A/B = 3/2 and B/G = 4/3. Twice A equals three B; three B equals four
G; therefore twice A equals four G, and A = 2G. This closes a two-interval path
against a direct doubling. XII identifies octave = 2, fifth = 3/2 and fourth =
4/3, and also concludes octave-plus-fifth = 3 and double octave = 4. Its musical
argument invokes X, VI and VII and the stated consonance assumptions. XIII uses
VIII to identify the difference between fifth and fourth as 9/8. XIV then invokes
the octave, tone and IX results to conclude that an octave is smaller than six
tones. Those prerequisite propositions and the introduction were read; the
complete *Elements* VIII.2 proof was not acquired here.

Thus one shared vocabulary must satisfy simultaneously:

- fourth × fifth = octave;
- fifth ÷ fourth = tone;
- tone^6 > octave, with the numerical example's exact discrepancy.

This is more restrictive than assigning fresh values to nodes of a directed
acyclic graph: routes meet, use the same interval meanings, and a purported
six-tone closure contradicts the source's inequality. A rival that treats six
tones as exactly one octave cannot preserve the fixed 9:8 and 2 ratios. Merely
renaming its equal steps does not repair that conflict. No universal consonance
law or claim about human hearing is inferred from this historical theory.

**Source and meaning limits.** Ruelle flags a restored proposition between VI
and VII, label/figure issues elsewhere, and corrections in later propositions.
The selected second proof of VI and complete IX need no repair of those figures.
ASCII number-line layouts were read as text, not measured as manuscript geometry.
The notation is a modern mathematical projection of the source's ratio claims.
It does not license six words, seven numbers, a literal note string, or a copied
proof order in an unknown text. Absolute pitch is free; uniform positive power
changes of all ratios preserve abstract multiplication/equality/order if the
numeric ratios themselves are not bound. Consistent identity renaming also
survives. A fitted unlabeled loop therefore would not identify an octave noun,
a sound-frequency unit or a historical instrument.
Permuting the same interval factors preserves the final ratio while generally
changing intermediate sounds. Endpoint closure alone therefore cannot recover
the order of a path; any claimed intermediate sequence needs its own binding.

## B. Simultaneous grouping and reconstruction of one object count

The prospective content is a complete account of counting the same collection
in several group sizes, retaining remainders, reconstructing a compatible total,
and checking or qualifying the answer. A single total must persist through all
groupings. Each stated quotient/remainder satisfies n = mq + r and 0 ≤ r < m.
Reconstruction and direct grouping are different paths to a common constraint;
an independently described total, if present, must agree with all of them.

The complete source is the *Sunzi suanjing* problem conventionally called
“objects of unknown number,” volume III. The
[complete Chinese text and Conway's translation](https://yawnoc.github.io/sun-tzu/iii/26)
were read, and the entire problem, answer, procedure and general rule were
independently viewed on **PDF page 74** of the
[public original print scan](https://archive.org/download/02094034.cn/02094034.cn.pdf).
The library contributor is Peking University Library via CADAL. The scan's
metadata names Li Chunfeng and others as commentators but supplies no securely
identified impression date; none is invented here. The work is commonly dated
approximately to the third–fifth centuries CE, as the
[translator's preface](https://yawnoc.github.io/sun-tzu/) reports. This is not an
independently acquired pre-1450 witness of the exact passage, or evidence that
the Voynich author had the work. No Chinese or phonetic target-language claim
follows.

**Every source-written action and assertion in the bounded problem:**

1. The number of objects is unknown. Grouping by 3 leaves 2; grouping by 5 leaves
   3; grouping by 7 leaves 2. The question asks the number.
2. The answer states 23. The question does not explicitly say “least.”
3. For those three remainder conditions, place 140, 63 and 30, respectively.
4. Add these quantities, obtaining 233.
5. Subtract 210 to obtain the answer.
6. A general rule states coefficients for remainder 1: use 70 for grouping by 3,
   21 for grouping by 5, and 15 for grouping by 7.
7. At 106 or above, subtract 105 to obtain the result.

The source does not explain how the three coefficients were discovered, why
105 is the common period, or a general arbitrary-modulus construction. It also
does not spell out a repeated-subtraction loop. These gaps are preserved rather
than silently repaired into a complete modern CRT theorem. A later modern
content compiler could explicitly declare such an extension, but it would be
an additional model assumption.

**Our exact closure checks, separate from written source values.** The coefficient
residue triples, ordered by moduli 3, 5, 7, are 70 → (1,0,0), 21 → (0,1,0), and
15 → (0,0,1). Consequently 2×70 + 3×21 + 2×15 = 233, and 233−210 = 23; direct
grouping of 23 returns (2,3,2). Each of the three direct division paths therefore
agrees with the source's reconstruction path. The quotient values 7, 4 and 3
are computed checks, not source-written entries in this problem.

A rival swaps the coefficients belonging to the first two group sizes while
preserving the coefficient multiset and given remainder numbers. It produces
282, whose representative 72 has remainders (0,2,2). The last grouping survives,
but the first two fail. Independent arbitrary quotient choices cannot fix that
under the same integer meaning and remainder bounds.

**Non-uniqueness retained.** The equations also admit 23+105k. For example 128,
233, 338 and 443 share the remainders. These are genuine alternative totals,
not failed arithmetic. The source's chosen 23 and reduction convention must
remain distinct from a modern assertion that the premises alone determine a
unique natural number. A complete account may explicitly select a representative
or add an upper bound; absent such content, those alternatives survive. There
is also no identified noun for the objects. Coefficients can be shifted by
common-period multiples without changing their residue action.
Reordering the three grouping clauses also preserves these arithmetic facts;
their order in a written account is not recovered from the endpoint equations.
