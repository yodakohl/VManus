## All five paragraphs, not just the favorable cooking passage

1. Most people eat cabbage as a relish; physicians use it as a drying medicine. Galen explicitly refers to earlier discussions and announces a summary. Its **juice purges**, whereas its **body checks evacuation by drying**. For evacuation, cabbage is taken with water from the nearby cooking pot and immediately put in serving vessels containing oil and fish sauce. Salt may replace fish sauce without changing the stated result.
2. To dry a moist belly, moderately boil, discard the **former water**, immediately transfer to **other hot water**, and boil again until soft. For evacuation, avoid thorough boiling so as to retain as much of the plant's own juice as possible. No boiled item retains all its juice; longer boiling loses more.
3. Lentil soup is compared with cabbage as capable of both gut outcomes. The preparation is called twice-boiled for both. The method extends to onion, leek, especially wild leek and garlic, and other things whose original nature one wishes to change toward its opposite. The twice-boiled material must touch neither air nor cold water: otherwise it no longer softens properly even after prolonged boiling. The earlier immediate transfer is explicitly repeated: have hot water ready, draw from the former water, transfer into that.
4. Lentil soup and cabbage dry similarly and therefore dull sight, **except when the whole eye is abnormally moist**. The exception does not explicitly promise improved sight. Lentil soup provides much, thick, melancholic nourishment; cabbage little, relatively moister nourishment because it is a vegetable rather than solid food. Unlike lettuce it has bad, foul-smelling juice. Galen says he cannot identify an obvious good or bad urinary consequence—an epistemic limit, not established absence of effect.
5. Galen criticizes people who call this vegetable *rhaphanos*, as though addressing Athenians six hundred years earlier. Contemporary Greeks use that name for **another plant**, unnamed in this entry. This naming polemic is part of the complete content, not disposable prose around a recipe.

## Coupled meaning and distinct counterreadings

The same plant supplies constituents assigned opposite effects. Two desired outcomes select preparation paths that retain or remove juice. That explanation also constrains the later reference chain: **former water**, **other hot water**, **in that**, **from the former**, **into that**. The second paragraph and the explicit reprise in the third must resolve these references consistently. Repeating the reprise is not an additional third boiling.

A model in which a second identical action merely flips an outcome while retaining all old water misses both disposal/replacement and the written reason for the change. A one-effect-per-plant model cannot retain both desired-outcome guards. A model that treats every repeated material or demonstrative as the same physical batch incorrectly identifies first and replacement waters. Similar drying also cannot force identical nourishment, because the entry explicitly contrasts lentil and cabbage nourishment.

This is more specific than assigning arbitrary signs to benefit and harm: the whole source explains why the alternatives differ, names what is retained or removed, resumes the transfer, gives a failure condition and limits other claims. The two paths are **alternative uses of a material type**; the entry does not narrate one physical serving undergoing both outcomes.

The source supplies no numerical amount, temperature, duration, loss rate or exact composition. The evacuation branch takes cabbage together with water, without saying all the water. The second branch does **not explicitly say to throw away the second water before eating**; that missing step must not be silently imported. The air/cold-water countercase explicitly predicts failure to soften, not a separately stated gut outcome. The air prohibition remains literally stated; its practical scope and truth are not repaired to a modern thermal explanation. Nor does the entry identify a chemical component whose mass must be conserved.
