# Geomancy source check: figure names with recurring formula/rubric text

Date: 2026-09-15
Scope: external historical sources only. No Voynich target data, images,
transcriptions, or new selectors were opened for this note. This is a source
and hypothesis note, not a target reading or a semantic confirmation.

## Verdict

A concrete historical precedent exists for a compact text that combines named
geomantic figures with repeated short house rubrics and figure-specific
judgements. The strongest primary evidence is Robert Turner's 1655 English
transcription of *Of Geomancy*, where the sixteen figure names are introduced,
the fifteen-position construction is described, and each figure is then
reused in a repeated `In the [n] House, [figure] ...` template. A second
primary witness, Theodoor Rodenburgh's 1617 play, puts the names **Rubeus** and
**Letitia** directly into short house judgement lines and links them to good or
bad outcomes.

This makes a geomantic-style 32-line page structurally plausible as a list,
heading, or rubric-bearing teaching/display text. It does **not** explain a
32-line count, identify a page layout, establish that any Voynich form is a
figure name, or bind a target line to a particular house or question. The
source relation is a historical possibility for a future hypothesis, not a
meaning anchor for the target.

The old claim that the 16 surviving GDT957 arithmetic charts represent
“different omitted figures” is excluded. The registered arithmetic correction
states that they all omit the same fixed value 0, **Populus**; they differ in
mother assignment. That fact supplies no semantic variation in the survivors.

### Named dictionary and construction

The source first says the art is founded on sixteen figures and gives their
names. It then pairs them with planets and contextual properties. The compact
name passage includes:

> “all Figures upon which this whole Art is founded are onely sixteen ... with
their names.”
>
> “Via, and Populus ... are referred to the Moone”; “Acquisitio, and Laetitia”
> are of Jupiter; “Puella, and Amissio” of Venus; “Conjunctio and Albus” of
> Mercury; “Puer and Rubeus” of Mars; “Carcer, and Tristitia” of Saturn; and
> “the Dragons head, and Dragons tayle”.

The same text describes a four-mother/four-daughter construction and then
four derived figures, two witnesses, and one judge. In its own terminology:

> “these foure Figures ... are wont to be called Matres”; the four secondary
> figures are “Filia, or Succedents”; four later figures are made from the
> ninth through twelfth; “two Figures which they call Coadjutrices, or Testes”
> are made; and one from those “is called the Iudex”.

These are source-level relations among names, generation roles, and positions.
They do not imply that a target list has those roles.

### Repeated house rubric: complete small Populus section

After defining the twelve house domains, Turner gives a repeated section under
`People`/`Populus`. Each house clause starts with the figure name and changes
the semantic domain with the house. The complete section is at source lines
64–76 of the Princeton transcription; representative exact openings are:

> “Populus being found in the first House ... sheweth a mean life ...”
>
> “In the second House, Populus sheweth a mean substance ...”
>
> “In the third House, Populus raiseth few friends ...”
>
> “In the fourth House, it signifies a father to be sickly ...”
>
> “In the fifth House, he sheweth no honest Messages ...”
>
> “In the sixth House, Populus sheweth cold sicknesses ...”
>
> “In the seventh House, it sheweth a wife to be fair and pleasant ...”
>
> “In the eighth House, it denotes sudden death ...”
>
> “Populus in the ninth House, sheweth false dreams ...”
>
> “In the tenth House he signifies such Kings and Princes ...”
>
> “In the eleventh House he giveth few friends ...”
>
> “In the twelfth House he sheweth weak and ignoble enemies ...”

The repeated formula is therefore not merely a modern summary: the historical
teaching text repeatedly places the same named figure into numbered house
rubrics, with a short judgement attached to each occurrence. It provides a
concrete model for a compact repeated-register page. It does not provide a
fixed 32-line serialization.

The source's full introductory house list also gives stable rubric domains:
first = querent/life; second = substance and gain/loss; third = siblings and
short journeys; fourth = fathers, patrimony, possessions and hidden things;
fifth = messengers, rumours, honour and pregnancy; sixth = infirmities,
medicines, servants and animals; seventh = marriage and disputes; eighth =
death, inheritances and dowry; ninth = journeys, faith, dreams and sciences;
tenth = honours and offices; eleventh = friends and princes; twelfth = enemies,
servants, imprisonment and misfortune.

This is a direct historical instance of named figures embedded in brief
house-rubric judgements: `Rubeus` is assigned to the fifth house and called
bad, while `Letitia` is named for the sixth house and called good. The nearby
text also uses figure point counts, a companion/adjacency relation, planetary
and zodiac associations, and a question/answer dialogue. It is especially
relevant to the requested possibility of repeated short formula lines, while
remaining a literary performance rather than a neutral geomancy manual.

The article's method summary (pp. 3–6, HTML lines 103–121) independently
describes sixteen parallel point rows, four `matres`, four `filiae`, four
`neptes`, two `testes`, and an `iudex`, with the same-pair point-combination

using recognizable geomantic terms in rapid positive/negative succession and
interprets “associatio” as immediate figure adjacency. The article also
warns that the scene is partly opaque and unorthodox. That warning is useful:
the existence of a named-rubric literary text does not make every occurrence
an unambiguous technical instruction.

## Relation to the 32-line-page question

The sources support four concrete historical ingredients that could, in
principle, be compressed into a teaching or display page:

1. a finite named figure inventory (sixteen names);
2. a generation diagram with fifteen common shield positions;
3. numbered house rubrics (twelve semantic domains); and
4. repeated figure-name + house + judgement clauses.

They do not support an exact claim that a 32-line page must contain 16
figure-name lines plus 16 formula lines, or that a particular target margin is
such a page. That mapping would require an independently visible ownership,
heading, or repeated textual binding. Without that binding, this source check
only raises a historically grounded hypothesis ceiling: a future reader may
model a compact named-rubric register, but may not report a translated word,
figure assignment, house assignment, or full page reading from this source
analogy alone.
