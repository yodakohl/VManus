# Source extraction: Aristotle, *Meteorology* I.9–12

This is a source-only extraction from the cached Webster translation
`ARISTOTLE_WEBSTER_I_9_12.txt` (Parts 9–12). The Corpus Thomisticum page is
used only as contextual confirmation of a medieval exposition: it presents
Thomas Aquinas's *Sentencia super Meteora* and a Leonine 1886 text. Neither
source is treated as a Voynich plaintext or as proof of a circa-1420 witness.

The account begins with a sun-driven cycle. Solar approach and retreat cause
dissipation and condensation. Heat evaporates earth-surrounding moisture and
raises it as vapour. As the lifting heat disperses or is quenched, the vapour
cools in the colder region, condenses from air into water, and falls back to
earth. Vapour is the water exhalation; cloud is air changing into water; mist
is the residue after cloud has condensed, and indicates fair weather more than
rain. The cycle reverses its vertical direction with the sun's position, but
its sequence remains fixed. Small falling drops are drizzle and larger ones
rain.

Low-rising daytime vapour cools and descends at night. If it condenses to
water without enough cold to freeze, it is dew; if it freezes before returning
to water, it is hoar-frost. Dew is favoured by warmth, frost by winter and
cold places, and both require clear sky and still air. Frost is absent on
mountains because its vapour rises from hollow, wet places and cannot be
lifted high, while stronger high-altitude air motion disperses it. Normally
south wind brings dew and north wind suppresses it. Pontus reverses this:
its south is too cool for sufficient evaporation, while north cold
concentrates heat by recoil; wells there emit more vapour in north wind.
Formed surface water is treated differently from cloud-region water.

Rain, snow and hail are upper-region bodies condensed by cold. Rain corresponds
to dew and snow to frost, with the upper forms involving more vapour, greater
collection area/time and quantity. Cloud freezing produces snow; vapour
freezing produces frost. Snow therefore indicates a cold season or country.
Hail is the deliberate exception: it has no lower-region counterpart.

Hail is ice formed when already formed water freezes faster than it can fall.
Its large angular stones and nearby loud clouds are used to infer a short fall
and freezing near the ground; long falls make stones smaller and rounder. The
source rejects the attributed theory that clouds rise into cold upper air and
freeze there: that would predict high hail, contrary to observation and to
snow's mountain distribution. It also opposes Anaxagoras's claim that violent
showers follow ascent into cold air; the account instead has cloud descending
into warm air, where surrounding heat concentrates cold by recoil. Rapid
condensation makes larger drops and violent showers; still faster freezing of
the formed water makes hail. Warmth previously given to water is said to
speed freezing. Hail is most common in spring and autumn, less common in late
summer, and rare in winter; summer dryness explains the pattern. Arabia and
Ethiopia are named exceptions where extreme heat produces repeated torrential
summer rain.
