## A. Following-word scope, with a same-head contrast

Cappelli section 5.62 explicitly gives attached superscript letters whose
characteristic letters belong to the following word, not simply to the end
of the base word. The printed table contains these source pairs:

| Printed construction locator | Printed expansion | Narrow consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Left column, fourth row: base h with superscript oo | hoc modo | One attached written construction represents two lexical words. |
| Left column, fifth row: base h with superscript on | hoc nomen | The same visible base h has a different following-word output when the attached letters change. |
| Right column, seventh row: marked ut head with superscript t | ut dicit | The added letter does not merely complete the written head. |
| Right column, eighth row: marked ut head with superscript r | ut probatur | Same head, different attached letter and different following word. |

The descriptions are navigation labels, not normalized machine glyph strings.
The page image owns the exact positions, bars and letter shapes; none is
removed by these descriptions. Row counts refer only to section 5.62's two
columns, starting with their first displayed example.

This bounds a useful negative requirement: a writer claimed to cover these
printed source examples cannot impose exactly one expanded lexical word per
attached written group. Conversely, these four examples do not establish an
unrestricted rule for inserting a word, nor prove that a detached oo always
means modo. The host and attached sequence must remain a joint finite license
until a shared decomposition is actually demonstrated. They give no license
to introduce hidden spaces anywhere in another manuscript.

The smallest present use is representational: preserve these four paired
examples as finite source relations when reviewing a candidate writer's
output-domain contract. A within-word-only writer is inadequate for that
declared source family; a source-scoped phrase-capable writer may represent
them. Neither outcome chooses a Voynich reading. No new source fitting or
search is selected merely to rediscover these printed examples.

## B. Countercase: superscript does not always abbreviate

Section 5.45, printed p33, gives patri with a raised final s as patris and
explicitly treats that s as the ordinary final letter, especially in the
twelfth century. The top of p35 also gives several single-word superscript
forms before the section 5.62 multiword table.

Thus elevation alone cannot determine either omitted letters or multiword
scope. A complete source inventory must keep the literal-letter branch as
well as bound abbreviation branches. The twelfth-century qualification is
retained; it is not a universal period-independent frequency assertion or
a claim about CoReMA usage. No new idea is needed for this countercase alone.
