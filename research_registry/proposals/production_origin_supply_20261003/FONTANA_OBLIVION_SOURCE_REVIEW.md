## Reading the complete operational unit

The Latin presents two useful alternatives: imagine old forms removed to
another locus; or treat them as sleeping/dead and establish another image over
them through stronger imagination and repeated meditation, disregarding the
old. The author then describes his own covering practice: hide the old image
behind a curtain or mat and create another on the near side, without obstruction.

Imagining the locus collapsing, burning, excessively dark or dazzling is
qualified as partly unhelpful because it also destroys the locus. These are
cautioned alternatives, not four equally recommended image-deletion methods.
The thesis prose's five-method summary loses this distinction.

The final Latin recommends taking care not to occasion image destruction,
which rarely occurs without difficulty. The thesis English instead describes
studying how to destroy images, reversing the negative purpose clause. Neither
sleep/death nor prior poor formation is established here as a diagnostic
criterion requiring deletion: sleeping/dead describes an imagined treatment.

Thus the passage distinguishes moving an existing image elsewhere from
installing a new image while preventing the old from obstructing attention.
Preserving a usable locus is a practical constraint; identical image identity
at its destination and later retrieval are not separately demonstrated.

## What this changes, and what it does not

A source-faithful hypothesis must keep at least three entities separate:
old image, new image, and locus. Moving old image A from locus L to another
locus M differs from installing B at/in front of L while covering A. They
cannot be collapsed into one generic CHANGE operation. Destroying L is a
warned cost, not an interchangeable implementation of replacing A. This is a
conditional operational formalization of the text, not evidence for physical
erasure, movable manuscript parts, or an actual sequence of written records.
