## Complete development entry V.1: Von der Wermuot

Heading: printed 380.29; body: 380.30–381.25. Complete marker-to-next-marker source-byte slice SHA256 `51cda980e56c548a50fab68207e885fc697232905096c6f9195b91fbad71a906`, 22,460 bytes. [Entry anchor](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/germ/mhd/konrmeg/konrmt.htm#Konr.Meg._Nat._V_1).

The entry names Absinthium as Wermuot, calls it bitter and useful to human nature, and attributes that account to Platearius and other masters. Its complete assertions are:

- **380.33–381.6:** a person drinks the herb's juice, mixed with wine or unmixed. The stated indications include bodily worms, obstruction of liver and spleen, a head illness attributed to bad vapor, anticipation of the falling illness named *apoplexia*, and lost speech. Wine is expressly optional within this branch; the text gives no separate wine-versus-unmixed outcome.
- **381.7–8:** a person with ear-worms puts drops of the juice into the ears. The juice and route are explicit; amount and frequency are absent.
- **381.8–12:** drinking the juice is said to clarify sight. The text also says it protects books, clothes and wood for many years from worms and mice, then says it strengthens the stomach and digestion. The pronoun in the artifact-protection assertion at 381.9–10 may continue the juice or refer more generally to the herb; the application operation is not written. Neither ambiguity supplies a covert washing or rubbing step. Stomach/digestion are human endpoints; books are not silently their recipient.
- **381.12–14:** roast Wermuot with oil, then anoint a person's body with that preparation; the stated endpoint is protection from fleas. The oil, roasting and application all belong to this branch.
- **381.14–16:** a scribe boils his ink with it and writes books or letters with that ink; the text says mice do not gnaw that writing. The first local anaphor may select the herb or the preceding preparation; its exact material composition is not resolved here. The writing is the protected recipient, and the scribe is the actor. This is not the body-anointing event with an incidental mention of books.
- **381.16–17:** some put Wermuot in their lye against mites. No laundering operation, concentration, substrate or time is specified.
- **381.17–25:** the herb is said to have two opposed properties: loosening/softening the person who needs that, and drawing together/making firm the person who needs the latter. The latter is attributed to coarseness of its juice, the former to heat and bitterness. The entry ends by assigning heat in the first degree and dryness in the second. These are source assertions and causal explanations, not measured classifications. The text does not prescribe two preparations that isolate the opposed properties.

There is therefore an actual preparation/recipient contrast with three stated operation chains: drink juice; roast with oil and anoint a body; boil ink with the locally referenced material and write. Their effects concern different recipients or hazards; the ink branch's material anaphor remains explicitly unresolved. The oppositional loosening/constricting passage additionally requires the recipient's stated need, but does **not** identify a preparation switch that would predict one of those two outputs. Both kinds of evidence must be retained rather than collapsed into a generic beneficial-herb value.

## Complete development entry V.2: Von dem Anetkraut

Heading: printed 381.27; body: 381.28–382.11. Complete source-byte slice SHA256 `aa86255428b36fa7be0b5be6cd148971bee0e7120cbf83953e91ca8ffb3b9e6e`, 14,505 bytes. [Entry anchor](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/germ/mhd/konrmeg/konrmt.htm#Konr.Meg._Nat._V_2).

The entry equates Anetum with Anetkraut and, citing Platearius, calls it hot and dry. Its full account preserves these separate branches:

- **381.29–30:** gather the herb in autumn and dry it. The ensuing bladder-stone assertion follows this preparation instruction, but a consumption/application route for that assertion is not expressly supplied.
- **381.31–33:** it is said to help against the named complaints `wüllen`, `undäw` and `heschen`, with the condition of holding it to the nose. These vernacular complaint labels are retained without replacing them by a modern diagnosis. The scope of that trailing condition over the whole coordinated list versus the final complaint is not resolved here.
- **381.33–382.1:** chewing it, or boiling and drinking it, is said to strengthen brain and stomach, remove bodily winds and help prepare/cook urinary fluid in the body. The alternative chewing versus boiling-and-drinking is written; a distinct output for each alternative is not.
- **382.1–4:** a complete morphological comparison to Venichel follows: similar leaves and flowers, shorter stem, yellow small flowers in a crown-like arrangement. These features are not extra ingredients or actions.
- **382.5–6:** it is said to mature/cook cold moisture in stomach and body and bring good sleep. There is no newly stated route in this sentence; it must not acquire one merely because an earlier branch had one.
- **382.6–7:** frequent eating is explicitly conditioned and is said to weaken sight. This cannot be omitted from a generally beneficial summary of the same herb.
- **382.7–9:** nursing women consume its seed in meat broth or otherwise; the stated effect is much milk. Seed, actor class and broth alternative belong to this particular branch, not automatically to every earlier use of the whole herb.
- **382.9–11:** the reader is warned not to regard Anetkraut as Aneiskraut; the latter is another herb treated later. The complete entry therefore contains an explicit exclusion on material substitution.

The sight endpoint differs from V.1's juice-drinking assertion, but the pair changes **material and consumption/frequency together**. It does not isolate a causal herb-only contrast, and no operation-independent “opposite plant polarity” may be inferred. Within V.2, route and frequency are explicit scope arguments, while some other assertions leave their route unstated.
