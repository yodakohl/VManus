## Finding

I found one **authorable but weakly identifying** natural reading path: the complete two-paragraph f9v entry as a Jacea/Viola-like herbal entry, with a first descriptive block and a second prescription block. Its useful evidence is unusually concrete: the admitted illustration has individual five-petaled blue/pale flowers and divided leaflike parts; a 1485 *Gart der Gesundheit* caption visibly places `yacea` with `freyschem krut` under a pansy-like image; and existing whole-form work distinguishes paragraph-opening `pchor` from line-initial, non-paragraph-opening `ychor`, while treating `chor` as a content/plant-part whole. Those constraints make a page-level C0 candidate more specific than a generic herbal-page label.

The candidate reading, stated only at the register level, is: **“Jacea/Viola-like entry; first paragraph gives materia or plant description; the second paragraph starts a use/preparation subsection; line-initial `ychor` continues an item or instruction sequence; `chor` is a plant-part/content item.”** This does not translate the 48 groups, decide which plant part `chor` names, or choose a recipe action. It is a coherent C0 frame, not a complete reading.

The same evidence also leaves the decisive alternative alive: `fochor` may be an entry address or heading for the pictured plant rather than a taxon name. The late Jacea/Viola equivalence cannot be projected into c.1420: earlier reported Jacea uses include cornflower, knapweed and scabious-like plants; the 1485 source's hot/moist III profile differs from Fuchs 1543's warm/dry profile; no second admitted Viola-like owner carries an independently readable `fochor`; and no source relation binds any f9v word to a Jacea trait. The original full-entry capacity decision explicitly retained these two observationally equivalent architectures. Thus the proposed frame is the best existing place to *write* a first plausible C0, but not a promising route to selecting a translation under today's source/data constraints.

## Source-only f25v contact follow-up: animal benefit versus plant contamination (2026-10-02)

This is a bounded historical comparison only. No Voynich image or GDT1142 file was opened, no target assignment was made, and nothing here reopens or changes GDT1142. ### Candidate animal-benefit reading: hare and hare's lettuce

The late-antique Pseudo-Apuleius *Herbarium*, entry CXIII, *herba lactuca leporina*, has the compact relation `Lepus aestate cum animo deficit aestu, haec herba remediat, ideo leporina dicitur`: in summer the hare is weakened by heat, this herb remedies it, hence its name. The passage names a non-wound animal recipient and a plant-to-animal benefit relation. It does **not** explicitly say the hare eats the herb, give a dosage, or state that an illustration's mouth contact depicts ingestion. Its text is therefore a use/benefit countermodel only when kept separate from the picture.

The British Library's official record for Harley MS 1585 dates the manuscript 1150–1174, locates *Lactuca leporina* at f.52r, and says the relevant manuscript images are currently unavailable: <https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/041-001958807>. A 1904 secondary plate caption says a hare is eating the plant, but that reproduction is not accepted here as a checked primary image. The official Laurenziana Teca record for Plut.73.16 (1201–1300) and its image service were also checked; the sample scan review did not yield a verified matching hare-contact page. I do not claim either witness supplies the requested image-text pairing.

### Candidate reverse-direction reading: salamander contaminates fruit

The Morgan Library's official Fountains Abbey Bestiary record identifies MS M.890, f.18r, as England, probably North Yorkshire, ca.1325–1350. Its curatorial folio description states that four salamanders occupy a tree and that the salamander's venom infects the fruit, potentially killing anyone who eats it; a man is shown beneath the tree. Official page and image locator: <https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/34/159535>; the museum's expanded page description: <https://www.themorgan.org/collection/bestiary/159535/24>. The text/image sequence, as catalogued, is animal enters/tree contact → fruit becomes poisonous → an eater is killed. It reverses the hare passage's direction: animal contaminates plant material, and a separate recipient is harmed. There is no wound, tether, or opponent in this account. It is a source-backed medieval danger/contamination pattern, not evidence that f25v depicts a salamander, fruit, or a poisoned human.

Access limit: the Morgan folio record and the official cataloged description are available, but its image download/zoom path currently resolves to a not-found page and the expanded Morgan page returns 403 to this retrieval route. I therefore have **not** independently inspected the f.18r pixels or transcribed its Latin. I preserve the institution's exact locator/date and describe the image only as the official catalog does; this is a lead, not a primary-pixel visual finding. I did not substitute third-party reproductions for that missing access.

### Rival predictions and smallest consequence

For a contact-only animal/plant scene, an animal-benefit hypothesis predicts a written plant → animal recipient/use relation (the hare model). A contamination/danger hypothesis predicts animal → plant or plant-part → downstream recipient harm (the salamander model). A third live possibility is a plant-entry emblem/owner with neither causal relation expressed. These are genuinely different historical scene graphs, but neither external witness tells us which one f25v encodes: f25v has no visible named species, wound, distinct downstream patient, or independently established consumption.

The only consequence supported here is to keep both causal directions available in exploratory interpretation and to forbid treating mouth contact alone as proof of either. The salamander case makes a plant-contamination route historically concrete beyond the already known wounded-hind and rue/weasel cases; it does not identify a f25v role or create a Voynich word assignment. No qualifying contact-only single-animal witness with fully checked primary pixels was secured within this bounded search. The hare witness lacks retrievable pixels; the salamander witness has catalogued four animals plus a human recipient, so it is not a literal one-animal visual match. No experiment, registry decision, or translation follows.
