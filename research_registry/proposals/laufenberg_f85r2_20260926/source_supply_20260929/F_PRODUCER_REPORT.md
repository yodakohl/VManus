## What separates whole names, parts and uses

**Dipsacus:** The entry locally assigns Dipsacon, Labrum/Carduum Veneris, officinal Virga pastoris and Carduus fullonum to its subject, explaining some names through water-holding leaves or textile use. These are Lonitzer's local equivalences only. Two forms are then contrasted: the cultivated white-flowered form versus a spontaneously growing purple-flowered form; the cultivated one has broader/more incised leaves and more prickly heads, the spontaneous one narrower/less incised leaves and less prickly heads. Do not silently correct this historical distinction to a modern taxonomic identification.

The crucial part wording is “in singulis caulibus, in cacumine capitulum unum”: **one head per caulis**, not one head per entire plant. The rooted whole-plant image shows multiple terminal heads. The head is called elongated, prickly and hedgehog-like, becoming white when dry. The uses distinguish worms found inside the head, leaf application, leaf juice, distilled water, root cooked in wine and water held on leaves. Worms put in a pouch and hung at the neck/arm are not the head itself. Leaf-held water is not automatically leaf juice or distilled water. The root preparation has its own application and storage claim. The word *semen* does not occur in this Dipsacus entry; calling its *capitulum* specifically a **seed head** adds an interpretation beyond the source's literal part term.

**Atractylis:** The entry compares it with Cnicus while describing terminal *capitula ... aculeata*, a pale flower and a thin, useless root. The useful preparation is stated as *folia, coma et semen* ground and drunk with pepper/wine; **coma is not silently equated with capitulum**. A reported holding-versus-laying-down condition for pain is retained as an attributed claim, not an observed treatment result. Some authors include Carduus benedictus as a second Atractylis species; Lonitzer explicitly leaves that to their judgment. That qualified report does not license an unconditional synonym merge.

**Carduus:** The author expressly introduces **two cultivated forms, one prickly and one not**, and later repeats that the counterpart differs by lacking prickles. He also treats a wild form separately, with incised prickly leaves and prickly heads. The class therefore cannot be defined simply as every member having the very same prickly structure. The description separately tracks leaves, stem, head, flower, seed and long root. Different organs carry different content; later uses include root preparation and root application. The entry's terminology and naming claims concern this author, not all historical uses of Carduus/Scolymos/Cinara.

The Strobilus explanation is a further counterexample to an overly sharp name/part dichotomy: a whole plant may acquire a name **because its head resembles a pine cone**. The text says *capitulum eius* when describing that likeness. This does not make every use of the resulting plant name a direct organ reference, and it does not make every head term a taxon name.
