## The actual two-clause relation and its ownership
The rounded-leaf, multi-stem growth in the right middle has **maiorona** at its lower left, and the speech cartouche at its lower right. Both placements surround that growth. The antlered animal and **Dictānus** occupy the separate scene below. The caption’s **sus** names a pig as addressee; it does not rename the adjacent antlered animal, and the distant bottom-left pig-like figure does not move the sentence to Aron.
Diplomatic Latin: **Abſtine ſus / nō tibi ſpiro**. Expanded: **Abstine sus, non tibi spiro**. The s in both sus and spiro is the long-s type, and the printed bar over nō expands n. *Abstine* is an imperative to refrain/keep away; its complement is not written. *Sus* supplies the vocative animal. *Tibi* carries that same addressee into the negative declaration, while *spiro* has an unexpressed first-person singular speaker. The plant is the likely speaker by cartouche placement, rather than an explicitly repeated grammatical noun. “I do not breathe for you” is conservative; a measured odor response, disgust or poisoning is not in the sentence.
The source-bound argument shape is therefore `imperative_refrain(addressee=sus)` together with `NOT breathe(speaker=I, for=same_addressee)`, with spatial `speaker≈labelled_plant(maiorona)`. A hypothetical construction retaining this content would need the same animal in the imperative and recipient slot, and would need to pay for the spatial speaker binding. Classification or a later consumer is not required to make these two clauses meaningful. The native text supplies neither an injury event nor a cure output. This is source analysis, not a proposed assignment to Voynich words.
The other readable Latin motto, **oderint dum metuant**, means “Let them hate, provided they fear.” Its plural haters/fearers are unnamed and the cartouche sits under the fiery large bird. It does not own a medicinal plant–recipient argument. The three Greek top cartouches remain unresolved rather than being turned into medical claims.
