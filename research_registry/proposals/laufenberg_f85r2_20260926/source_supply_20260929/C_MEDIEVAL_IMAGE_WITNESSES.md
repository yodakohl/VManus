### 1. Positive contact witness: Harley MS 4751, f.14v

The [current British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002050594) dates the manuscript to the late twelfth–early thirteenth century (indexed start/end 1160–1240), with possible origin Salisbury, southwestern England. This is a manuscript date range, not a precise date for this miniature. The official [IIIF manifest](https://bl.digirati.io/iiif/ark:/81055/vdc_100056056799.0x000001) owns the canvas labelled `f.14v` and image `ark:/81055/vdc_100056056801.0x000024`.

Both the complete 1500-pixel page and the complete 3000-pixel page were inspected; no contact crop was used. In the [complete larger page](https://bl.digirati.io/images/ark:/81055/vdc_100056056801.0x000024/full/3000,/0/default.jpg), the caption is *Caprea*. A bowman stands at left. An upper quadruped bounds away with an arrow shaft in its flank. A lower quadruped bends its head to the green plant at lower right; its muzzle directly meets the plant. The lower animal also retains a horizontal shaft. A separate pointed form at its abdomen is visible, but its attachment and direction do not independently establish an object moving out of the animal. Neither a complete exit nor a healed final state is unambiguously shown.

Working normalized transcription from the text immediately below the miniature, expanding abbreviations: `Herbas ruminat. vulnerata ad dictamnum currit. qua tacta sanatur.` This is a working reading of the primary image, not a critical edition. The injury clause supplies order and the following clause asserts healing through contact. The preceding rumination sentence is general animal description; it must not be spliced into the dittany clause to manufacture an explicit statement that the wound is cured by eating. The upper and lower figures can be read as two episodes of one actor, but actor continuity is an interpretation of the composition, not an independently demonstrated identity.

**Consequence:** a conditional whole reading with a wounded animal approaching/touching a healing plant has a dated medieval image-and-text analogue. The stronger Pliny chain in IDEA745, eating → weapon falls out, remains a distinct candidate. The picture supplies direct mouth contact; the text supplies prior injury and healing. Neither alone proves ingestion or expulsion. This is also a bestiary witness, not automatically a herbal-page owner.

### 2. Same contact story can be illustrated without contact

[BL Add MS 11283](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002109041), f.7r, is dated by the institution to the fourth quarter of the twelfth century (1175–1199), England. [Official manifest](https://bl.digirati.io/iiif/ark:/81055/vdc_100055965341.0x000001); canvas label `f.7r`; image `ark:/81055/vdc_100055965343.0x000016`.

The [full page](https://bl.digirati.io/images/ark:/81055/vdc_100055965343.0x000016/full/1500,/0/default.jpg) shows an isolated *Caprea* drawing and a separate *Monoceros* below. There is no plant at the Caprea's mouth and no visible wound or projectile. The Caprea paragraph nevertheless contains the corresponding wounded-animal/dittany/contact-healing account. This comparison is not a quantitative study of copying: it establishes only that the presence of this text does not require its action to be drawn on every surviving page.

### 3. Therapeutic danger is different from consumption: Trinity O.2.48

The [institutional James catalogue](https://mss-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk/Manuscript/O.2.48) identifies this Latin herbarium as fourteenth century, possibly German. [Official manifest](https://mss-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk/Manuscript/O.2.48/manifest.json).

Foliation must be kept explicit. The James catalogue's 19a/19b/20a descriptions correspond in the examined images to manifest labels `f020r`/`f020v`/`f021r` respectively. Modern numerals 20/21 and older xix/xx make this local shift visible. This three-page correspondence is verified; it is not a claim that the entire manuscript follows a uniform offset.

- **Manifest f020v, older/catalogue 19b:** [full page](https://mss-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk:8183/iiif/2/O.2.48%2F051_O.2.48_f020v.jp2/full/1500,/0/default.jpg). A seated physician holds up a cup; a standing human raises both hands; a long serpent overlaps/passes around the human's ankles. A large flowering plant occupies the lower portion. Neither the serpent's mouth nor the patient's mouth contacts the plant. The serpent does not visibly bite the patient; the cup does not visibly reach the patient's mouth. The lower rubric is readable as `Ad serpentis morsus` (normalized working reading). Thus text and scene support a snakebite-treatment context, while actual biting, swallowing and causal success remain unstated by the picture alone.
- **Manifest f021r, older/catalogue 20a:** [full page](https://mss-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk:8183/iiif/2/O.2.48%2F052_O.2.48_f021r.jp2/full/1500,/0/default.jpg). A mounted hunter's lance reaches an animal's neck, with a red wound; a foot archer and hounds accompany the hunt. Vegetation is present, but no animal mouth touches it. The page must not be described as an image of an animal eating dittany merely because it lies within the dittany entry. The extended text has not been diplomatically collated here, so a stronger expulsion claim is not based on this page.
- **Manifest f020r, older/catalogue 19a:** [full page](https://mss-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk:8183/iiif/2/O.2.48%2F050_O.2.48_f020r.jp2/full/1500,/0/default.jpg). A physician offers a cup to a man holding his abdomen, with another attendant at right. This is the preceding medical scene, not the snakebite picture.
- **Manifest f019v:** [full page](https://mss-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk:8183/iiif/2/O.2.48%2F049_O.2.48_f019v.jp2/full/1500,/0/default.jpg). Botanical figures, without an animal or human action. Initially retrieved because the catalogue's older folio number was mistaken for the manifest label; retained as a transparent locator correction.

**Consequence:** the already present source family distinguishes at least two role structures: an animal as wounded plant-user, and an animal as a dangerous cause relevant to a human treatment. A pictured serpent does not automatically own either the consuming or the benefiting role. The correct contrast is between complete accounts with participants and order, not a name assigned to the pictured creature.

### 4. Rue/protection: conditional Worcester witness, plus two negative picture checks

The [Rijksmuseum object RP-F-2001-7-479-17](https://id.rijksmuseum.nl/200652719) is an album page with two photographs of Worcester Cathedral carvings, marked XXXIII and XXXIV. The official record dates the photographic object to about 1863–in or before 1873. That is **not** the date of the carving. The [cathedral's own overview](https://www.worcestercathedral.org.uk/heritage/architecture/misericords) says that 39 of its 42 misericords date from the fourteenth century; it does not identify this particular carving among those 39. The exact carving date has not been resolved in this bounded investigation.

The complete [official Rijksmuseum image](https://iiif.micr.io/bBVLy/full/max/0/default.jpg), rather than only an animal's mouth, was inspected. Upper photograph XXXIII shows a central bird-headed, winged creature with a coiled serpent-like tail, flanked by two quadrupeds facing inward. Leaf-like sprigs meet/hang at their mouths. The picture supports animals carrying or holding foliage in the presence of another creature. It does not independently show swallowing, attack, immunity, or an earlier and later scene. The lower photograph XXXIV is a different carving and must not be merged into the event.

The Rijksmuseum description cautiously calls the flanking animals possible wolves. The familiar weasel-and-rue identification is an iconographic interpretation; plant and animal taxonomy are not determined by this image inspection. This makes the carving useful for an explicitly conditional protective-herb reading, but not an independently dated demonstration of the precise Pliny sequence in IDEA744. No modern photo date is being smuggled in as medieval provenance.

Two inspected manuscript pictures do not resolve the missing sequence:

- [Harley MS 4751 f.31r](https://bl.digirati.io/images/ark:/81055/vdc_100056056801.0x000045/full/1500,/0/default.jpg), same dated manuscript and manifest as above, image `ark:/81055/vdc_100056056801.0x000045`: a roundel under *Mustela* shows an elongated quadruped confronting a bird-headed scaled creature. No visible herb or mouth–plant action. The scene is not a rue-consumption witness merely because the opponent and animal suit a familiar story.
- [BL Sloane MS 4016 f.63r](https://bl.digirati.io/images/ark:/81055/vdc_100165751215.0x00007f/full/1500,/0/default.jpg), institutionally dated about 1440 (1435–1445), Lombardy: [catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002116409), [manifest](https://bl.digirati.io/iiif/ark:/81055/vdc_100165172997.0x000001), image `ark:/81055/vdc_100165751215.0x00007f`. A large plant at left and a separately captioned small quadruped at right have a clear gap between them. No feeding or opponent is drawn. An online description of a plant and weasel cannot substitute for contact geometry.

The original primary textual mechanism for IDEA744 remains Pliny, *Natural History* XX, chapter 51 in [Bostock/Riley Volume IV](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/61113/61113-h/61113-h.htm), headed *Rue: eighty-four remedies*: an animal takes rue before its encounter with serpents. The source opponent is a serpent; replacing it with a basilisk is an additional historical identification, not a translation of the same sentence.

### 5. Nerium/recipient reversal: medieval plant witness, no event picture

[The Morgan Library's M.652 f.143r](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/259/143825), *De materia medica*, is catalogued as mid-tenth-century Istanbul (historical Constantinople) and identified as rhododaphne/oleander. The [complete institution image](https://ica.themorgan.org/icaimages/6/m652.143r.jpg) shows a single plant with Greek writing, without a quadruped, human, preparation or event. Its presence is evidence of a medieval illustrated plant entry, not a picture of recipient-specific poisoning or antidotal preparation. The Greek therapeutic text has not been collated here.

IDEA746's Pliny mechanism therefore remains textual: harmful effects for quadrupeds and a differently prepared compound associated with human protection. This investigation did not find the complete recipient-and-preparation reversal visibly expressed in its inspected image. That is a limit of this bounded set, not a claim that no such medieval picture or predecessor exists.

## Smallest useful next reading

The new source distinction is executable without another source corpus: preserve **Harley f.14v** as the wounded-animal/contact/healing account and **Trinity manifest f020v** as the human-treatment/danger account. On an already admitted target, root can now write complete conditional alternatives with explicitly provisional participants. Each account must state (1) who has the injury or faces danger, (2) who touches/uses the plant or preparation, and (3) which outcome follows. The same assumed participant must remain the same across clauses. Contact/healing, ingestion, projectile expulsion, danger treatment, and recipient-specific harm remain separate consequences.

No prior independent confirmation of every target role is required for this exploratory exercise. Its value is that the whole account has consequences beyond an animal or plant name. Observed target geometry can favor or strain a conditional account; it cannot silently convert a conjectured creature name into a confirmed lexeme. If no coherent whole account can be written, this source result still remains a positive historical fact, without implying a Voynich match.
