# Bounded source check: honey under recipient and seasonal conditions

## Finding

The bounded search did not locate a honey-specific passage in the already-owned Heinrich Laufenberg 1491 source reports. It did locate a strong, separately-provenanced comparator in the full English translation of entry 86, “Honey,” from Symeon Seth’s *Syntagma de alimentorum facultatibus*. The translation is included in Alison Noble’s document *Foodstuffs in Symeon Seth’s Syntagma de alimentorum facultatibus* (PDF, printed p. 7; complete entry, not a clipped quotation).

The entry identifies honey as hot and dry in the second degree, describes bitterness and bowel effects, and distinguishes preparation: honey mixed with plenty of water and fermented until foamy is more diuretic and easier to digest, with foam removed; honey boiled alone is less bitter and more nourishing. It says honey suits the elderly and, generally, cold temperaments, while people in their prime with hot temperaments develop bile. It recommends spring-harvested honey and says winter-harvested honey is unusable because it readily turns to yellow bile, especially where the intestines are hot. It also names a benefit for appetite in people anorexic from phlegmatic humors and an expectoration effect. The same entry preserves drawbacks: honey preparations can be hard to digest and, if poorly made, obstruct liver and spleen; immoderate continuous use causes itching. These qualifications make the parallel materially stronger than a generic honey praise or simple quality label.

## Scope and interpretation

This is an actual bounded food-entry translation attributed to the named medieval work, but the retrieved document supplies no Greek text, manuscript witness, critical-edition reference, or translation apparatus. I therefore treat it as a complete translated primary-source passage with a provenance limitation, not as an independently collated reading of a medieval witness. I did not verify whether the exact entry occurs in the Augsburg Laufenberg print or a southern-German housebook; this comparison remains a separate textual tradition. The source supports the historical availability of a recipient- and season-conditioned honey regimen, not a target reading, a causal model validated by experiment, or a direct line of influence on Laufenberg.

The contrast is not simply “cold people benefit, hot people are harmed”: age, life-stage wording, honey harvest season, intestinal heat, dilution/fermentation, boiling, preparation quality, and continuous excess all matter. The source does not state a phlegmatic-versus-bilious binary exactly as posed in the selection question: it explicitly connects anorexia from phlegmatic humors with appetite stimulation, and explicitly connects prime age/hot temperament and hot intestines with bile. Those links should not be collapsed into a single one-to-one temperament mapping.

No Voynich text, image, reserve, or draft/result was accessed. No semantic assignment or execution claim is made.

## Decision effect

This named comparator supplies a concrete historical precedent for conditional food evaluation: the same food has recipient-, season-, preparation-, and excess-sensitive consequences within one complete bounded entry. It makes a recipient-conditioned honey hypothesis historically intelligible. It does not establish that the more specific Laufenberg source has this rule, that any given target form means honey or a recipient condition, or that a modern physiological mechanism is involved. The original provenance question remains open for Laufenberg itself.
