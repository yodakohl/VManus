# Personified speech in the Augsburg 1491 year cycle

Source-only comparison prompted by IDEA000538. The eight newly inspected images were the complete official HAB scans 00161–00168, acquired and viewed whole. They establish the printed boundaries and contain Spring, Summer, and the start and body of Autumn. The complete Winter and physician units are reported from the two previously frozen source packets: Winter extension scans 00169–00171 and regimen/physician scans 00171–00175. Neither packet was reopened, repaired, or rewritten here. This is not a target reading or a claim that this print is the source of any manuscript.

## Boundaries and source sequence

The official HAB structural TEI metadata places the fourth chapter, “Wie das jare in vier teyl geteylt ist,” at scan 00161. Its opening overview names the parts in order: first *Glencz* (Spring), then Summer, Autumn, Winter. The whole-page scans confirm that the transition headings occur on pages that also carry neighboring verses: Spring starts with its named figure and picture on 00162 and closes on 00164 before Summer's heading; Summer is headed and begins on 00164 and closes on 00167 before Autumn's heading; Autumn is headed on 00167, begins its first-person caption and main verses on 00168, then has six concluding verses on 00169 before the Winter rubric. That last page and the Winter sequence are covered in the fixed Winter packet, whose report records the preceding season’s six-line close, cold-water warning, and comparison of the world’s changed appearance to a woman losing colour/beauty and fine clothes. I rely on that fixed summary for the Autumn close and make no new claim about its individual words. Winter begins under its explicit rubric at 00169, has its first-person voice and main verses across 00170–00171, and is followed on 00171 by the separate rubric about how a person should conduct himself according to the season. The fixed physician/regimen unit then runs to the new complexion heading on 00175.

## Whole-unit sense inventory

The short native phrases below are retained to show the actual speaker frames; spelling is lightly normalized only in surrounding prose. They do not constitute a critical edition.

1. **Spring / *Glencz* (00161–00164).** The chapter’s opening says it will divide the year into four parts and twelve months. It names Spring as the first season; the figure says “Ich haiß das glencz” (“I am called Spring”) and describes itself as gaily adorned with flowers. A woman holding a flower accompanies this printed text. The continued poem names March as its arrival and describes the season as warm and moist, with a change in blood during this time. It then addresses the reader directly (`dir`/`du`): moderate eating and drinking, proper regimen, and letting/purging are advised; several foods and further cautions follow. Spring therefore combines first-person seasonal self-naming, seasonal self-description, and reader-directed instruction. The body of the poem moves between the season as topic and the addressee as agent; the change is not inferred merely from a paragraph break.

2. **Summer (00164–00167).** Its printed transition calls it the year’s second part. The speaker says “Ich heiß der summer” and compares itself to a beautiful woman. The poem locates its beginning in May and names a span through a saint’s day; the transition page then describes its nature as strongly warm and dry. A woman with flowers accompanies the opening. Subsequent verses address the reader about conduct, food/drink and bodily care, including caution about heat and illness; a later line directs the reader to seek a cool dwelling. The first-person name/comparison is concentrated at the opening, while the long continuation is largely seasonal description and second-person advice. No claim is made that the spoken advice is literally issued by an independently identified human character.

3. **Autumn (00167–00169).** The third-part rubric and a woman holding a flower appear at the end of the Summer page. At the top of 00168 the voice says “Ich bin der herbst” (“I am Autumn”), has fruit, and declines in strength. The main lines date its arrival after the preceding season and give a seasonal span, then describe cold/dry qualities and melancholic change; the text shifts into advice about fruit, making the body clean, drinks and moderation. The last six lines on 00169 are documented only through the frozen Winter packet: its native observer reports a cold-water warning followed by a comparison of the world to a woman losing colour/beauty and fine clothing. The fixed packet places the Winter rubric after those lines. That visible fixed boundary supplies the end of Autumn; its summary is not silently expanded into a new transcription here.

4. **Winter (00169–00171; fixed packet).** The Winter voice says “Ich haiß der winter, sag ich dir” (“I am called Winter, I tell you”) and speaks of what is fruitful resting within it. The following verses give the season’s cold/moist complexion and permissively connect it with consuming more food, but do not state an internal-heat mechanism. They include a separate warming-consumable permission, health/necessity conditions, moderate bathing and warmth advice, and an old-woman comparison with an adverse age/death ending. The whole boundary, uncertainties and all lines are recorded in `HEAT_WINTER_EXTENSION_REPORT.md`; this packet is retained as fixed evidence.

5. **Physician/regimen voice (00171–00175; fixed packet).** A new rubric says to observe how a person should conduct himself according to the time/season. The speaker then says he learned his art from God and has sustained many people through him. A physician with a flask accompanies the text on 00172. The ensuing speaker teaches the reader about changing seasonal and bodily natures, contrasting what can be beneficial at one time and harmful at another, and gives qualifications for illness, health, age, and judgment. It is an explicitly instructional physician/author frame, not a fifth season. The complete continuation and qualifications are in the frozen `HEAT_PRINT_PARALLEL_REPORT.md`.

## Comparison and decision

Across the four seasonal figures there is a real repeated opening pattern: a part-of-year rubric or transition, first-person self-naming, and a short self-description tied to the season. Spring says it is called Spring and is flower-adorned; Summer names itself and compares itself to a beautiful woman; Autumn says it is Autumn and describes fruitfulness and waning strength; Winter names itself and describes what rests within it. The year sequence is explicitly stated and matches the order of the four figures. A reader is then addressed in second person for practical guidance, although the proportion and placement of such instruction vary. The poems are therefore not simply four identical “I am X” declarations: Spring’s self-description opens a longer reader regimen, Summer and Autumn put their names at the beginning but move among descriptions and advice, and Winter has a short address embedded in its self-naming (`sag ich dir`) before the longer instructional body.

The physician unit repeats first-person authority plus direct reader instruction, but its content and framing differ: it names an art learned from God and a human teaching role, and its rubric explicitly instructs a human about conduct through time. This supports a broad source-level distinction between personified seasonal voices and a physician/author speaker, while showing that first-person and second-person forms alone do not identify a speaker type. The source can motivate testing a repeated deictic/actor construction across varied contents; it does not license assigning any manuscript figure or text to Spring, Summer, Autumn, Winter, or physician. No target dictionary value, speaker identity, season count, or decipherment is confirmed.

**Decision:** the printed seasons use a stable, locally repeated first-person seasonal self-naming opening plus variable reader-directed instruction. The physician uses the same broad first-person-plus-addressee resources but has a distinct professional/instructional frame. Thus the source evidence is partly uniform in grammar and heterogeneous in speaker role, content, and instructional organization. Whether any target construction behaves this way remains a separate, falsifiable target-side question.
