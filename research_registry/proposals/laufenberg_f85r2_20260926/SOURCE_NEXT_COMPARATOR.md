## 1. Primary comparison: Cgm 5185

The KdiH catalogue identifies a late-fifteenth-century verse adaptation associated with Albrecht von Eyb, from Hartmann Schedel's library. Its compact closing sequence includes a physician (15r), Fortune (15v), three personified times (16r), Death (17r), and cosmic spheres (18r), followed by a ship image (18v). The times use figures of different ages but mean present, future and past. The catalogue dates the manuscript to 1486; the BSB record gives the fourth quarter of the fifteenth century and northern Bavaria. Thus it is not an early-fifteenth-century Upper Rhine witness. [KdiH 11.4.32](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/11/4/32); [BSB record and digitization](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/view/bsb00003708).

## 2. A concrete celestial-medical rival: Mercury

KdiH identifies **Tübingen UB Md 2, 271r** as Mercury with urinal, astrolabe and book, surrounded by zodiac/constellation figures and with occupations below. It places the planetary pictures on rectos, associated with text on versos. This is a different unit from the already inspected 23r, 43r and 322v. [KdiH 11.4.43](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/11/4/43); [exact canvas 544, label 271r](https://opendigi.ub.uni-tuebingen.de/opendigi/Md2/canvas/544).

Native whole-page inspection confirms a seated central figure in a circular enclosure, a raised circular instrument, a vessel in the other hand, a book on the lap, and a coiled creature below. There is a star in the enclosure. Other figures occupy the blue upper field, while working people, a teaching scene, books and tools fill the green lower field. There is **no running paragraph, annular text or four-sector division** on this recto. The professions and celestial identities rely on the catalogue; the page's visible objects alone do not prove every proper name.

The useful constraint is narrow: an apparent physician with a urine vessel can be a celestial personification within this manuscript tradition. That makes “urine vessel therefore a medical chapter author” nonexclusive, just as “staff therefore Winter” was nonexclusive. It does not identify a Voynich figure as Mercury and does not explain the other three target figures. The complete associated verse/prose has not been read here; this is not yet a complete source-bound content scenario.

A separate catalogue witness is **Edinburgh, Royal Observatory Cr. 4.6, old 9.14.5.14, 104v**, where Mercury is again described as a urine-examining physician in a planetary sequence. The manuscript is dated mainly around 1470 and localized to southwest Germany; its calendar points to Constance, with an Alsatian origin proposed. This is another witness to the convention, not proof of independent invention or textual lineage. Its image was **not** inspected: the catalogue's UNC MacKinney link and attempted search endpoints returned the website shell rather than an exact photograph. [KdiH 87.2.5](https://kdih.badw.de/datenbank/handschrift/87/2/5); [UNC collection](https://dc.lib.unc.edu/cdm/landingpage/collection/mackinney).

## 3. Ring text as a whole-diagram statement: Caius 428/428

The holding institution identifies **Gonville & Caius MS 428/428, Tractatus de quaternario**, as twelfth century, probably northern France. It connects the work's physiological account with humours, ages and seasons, and identifies the figure on folio 47 with four solar/zodiac turning points. These are institutional descriptions, not a newly transcribed whole treatise. [Gonville & Caius, The four winds](https://www.cai.cam.ac.uk/discover/library/online-exhibitions/safe-return-doubtful/four-winds).

The institutional gallery photograph was inspected natively. “47” is visible; a recto/verso suffix is not established by the exhibition's wording, so none is invented here. The lower half has four seated figures inside a circle quartered by diagonal bands. Each sector has local writing. Further writing follows a continuous outer circumference. A substantial prose block occupies the upper half. There is no visible urine flask, physician inspecting a patient, or distinct old person with a staff. There are also no four separate figure-adjacent prose paragraphs.

The institution explains the outer verse as the solar circuit forming the year. This is a real example where the ring concerns the ensemble and local inscriptions qualify its members. It supports a **layout distinction**, not a Voynich word assignment: local figure text and an annular statement need not enumerate the same objects or have the same grammatical role. It does not supply the mixed physician/age roles. The source is substantially earlier and outside the proposed Upper Rhine setting; likeness would establish neither copying nor geographical origin.
