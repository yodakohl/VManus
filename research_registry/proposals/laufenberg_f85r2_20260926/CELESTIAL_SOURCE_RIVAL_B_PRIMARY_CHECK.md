The text says *in tantum posset procedere quod ... iam tenderent in occasum*, followed by *quanto magis accederet ad austrum, tanto plus moverentur in occasum*: a person could proceed far enough that stars formerly of perpetual appearance would then tend/move toward setting, and the farther southward approach, the more they would move toward setting. This is qualitative, modal and comparative; it does not specify a distance or say that the stars must complete a setting. The review's phrase “tend to set after sufficient travel” should be read in this qualified sense, not as a guaranteed transition.

The remaining pivotal wording is direct: C2 calls it *una ... eadem eclipsis Lunæ numero* and says it appears to us *in prima hora noctis* and to easterners *circa horam noctis tertiam*, with their night and sunset earlier. This preserves one eclipse numerically, while the local hour is approximate; no instant/contact is named. For C5, *Ille iterum idem homo posset videre* explicitly resumes the same man as able to see the stars formerly of perpetual concealment; the following *Et e converso* gives the reverse-direction case with *alicui*, not necessarily that same individual. These clauses do not make the C4 and C5 stellar objects identical. The text states each flatness conditional with its rejected consequence, then closes that Earth appears flat *præ nimia eius quantitate hominum visui*—because of its excessive size to human sight.
