## Concrete complete source synopsis

The paragraph calls thunder the first of the discussed impressions of double
vapour and locates its generation in the watery substance of a cloud. Its
account describes hot, dry vapour moving and shaking while fleeing its
unnamed contrary, constrained on every side and striking into itself. Thereby
it is set on fire and flame and finally quenches itself in the cloud; the
account cites Aristotle. The exact vapour/fire antecedent remains compressed.

The paragraph also describes what happens when strong storm winds enter
clouds: the storm and whirling wind increase, seek a passage, split the cloud,
burst out strongly and break its parts. Dreadful breaking and noise reach
human and animal ears. This is said to be unsurprising: even a light bladder
makes a great noise if strongly inflated and afterward violently broken.
The bladder is an explanatory conditional example, not the same object as
the cloud and not a reported performed trial.

Lightning accompanies thunder. It is seen sooner because it is clear and
bright; thunder reaches our ears later, with the greater subtlety of sight
than hearing offered as the explanation. The text compares this order with
a person seeing a woodcutter's stroke before hearing the noise of that stroke.
This preserves the stated historical reasons and the stroke's ownership of
its noise. It does not assert simultaneous production, equal distances,
modern propagation speeds or identity of the observer with the woodcutter.

This synopsis has source-specific consequences: turning the search for an
exit into an exit already achieved loses a step; making a light bladder quiet
reverses the concession; treating the conditional bladder as an observed event
changes modality; making hearing earlier reverses the comparison; assigning
the sound to a different stroke loses an explicit reference. These differences
remain meaningful without inventing target words or a new semantic primitive.

## Which relations genuinely recur

**Access order has two distinct argument uses.** Weather pairs seeing lightning
with hearing thunder; woodcutting pairs seeing a stroke with hearing the noise
of that stroke. The visual and auditory objects are related but not simply
identical in either example. Same-stroke ownership is explicit in the latter.
Weather accompaniment supports related phenomena, not an equally explicit
single-event identity. The weather observer is expressed passively/generically
and through our ears; the woodcutting comparison explicitly retains one
perceiving person. These differences constrain a reusable account.

**Violent rupture and noise also have a written comparison.** Cloud breaking
has an auditory result; a strongly inflated bladder makes loud noise when
subsequently broken violently. The source explicitly uses the latter to make
the former unsurprising. The shared part is rupture/noise under forceful
conditions, not every premise. Only the bladder is expressly called light and
strongly inflated. The cloud account includes entry, increasing storm,
seeking a passage and broken parts. No universal pressure or acoustics law
is supplied.

**Explanatory linkage, condition and order recur at broader levels**, but they
do not all instantiate one mechanism. Self-striking→ignition, brightness→earlier
seeing and faculty subtlety→sensory-order explanation have different stated
grounds. The bladder's afterward orders described events; sooner/later orders
perceptual access. Ordinary clauses can express these relations. Their recurrence
is a source-content resource, not evidence for an elaborate state machine or
for a uniquely shared constructor.
