## Boundary and high-level reading

The selected unit begins below the prior Winter-ending stanza on scan 00171. The rubric “Hie merck wie sich ein mensch ye / nach der zeitte soll / halten vnd ist ein hüpsche / lere” introduces the following speech; I treat the four short printed lines as rubric, not as four verses of the unit. The two verse lines at the foot of 00171 begin the speech. It runs through eight lines at the top of 00175 and ends before the separate rubric “Hie merck wie alle menschen / nach den vier elementen sein / naturet vnd heissent die / vier comple[r]ion.” The following complexion material is outside the 64-verse unit.

The opening first-person speaker says the art was learned from God and that God made the speaker a physician (exact second-line wording partly uncertain). The discourse then says the year has four kinds/natures and bodies change according to times. It gives nonrational animals as models of adaptation; the first bird-list wording is uncertain. It explicitly contrasts a rich/healthy person with others and states that what is good for one can hurt another. Old and young are said to change differently with cold and heat. The poem appeals to wise/experienced physicians, then names the cost of human life, celestial-star and elemental forces, invisible spirits, sin, God's goodness/protection, and concludes that God gave the arts/teaching for the support of body and soul. It does not state a directional age-to-heat ranking or a fixed unchanged person whose response changes only by season.

For the crucial contrast, the grammatical subject of “das dem andern we thut” is anaphoric/underspecified in these lines: the text does not name one specific food or treatment as the shared evaluated object. It does state a generic “one / another” benefit–harm contrast and frames the passage by times and differing bodily constitution. The immediate preceding “Der reiche und der gesunde / hand ungleiche stunde” is itself hard to map exactly onto the contrast; I do not resolve whether it coordinates recipient kinds, life-times, or both. The modal force is gnomic/advisory in the “soltu” lines and more general/indicative in the benefit–harm aphorism; no single explicit if/then conditional governs every verse.

## Contrast and unresolved readings

The reciprocal content is plainly generic: the same deictic pattern “dem einen / dem andern” is used, but the thing that benefits or hurts is an anaphoric “das,” not a named item in these lines. The passage surrounds that claim with changing years/bodies and different times, yet this does not grammatically assert that one patient's constitution remains invariant while time alone changes. “Der reiche und der gesunde” also leaves unclear whether the two descriptions denote separate recipient classes, contrasting conditions, or loosely coordinated examples. The sick/healthy distinction is not developed into an explicit reciprocal pair on these pages; the clear line reads “Der reiche und der gesunde,” while earlier 00171 belongs to the preceding Winter stanza and includes a question about whether one is healthy, followed by the old-woman/death comparison. The following regimen unit does not retroactively turn that Winter line into part of this speech.

Animal examples are visibly present but exact taxa/verbs are not secure in lines 17–24. I read a group including a stork and swallows; “Der rey” and the winter clause need critical collation before lexical claims. The change thesis is clear at the level “year ... fourfold natures” and “bodies ... change their natures,” but the print does not here assign which age, season, or named temperament corresponds to which direction of heat/cold. Lines 45–54 attribute the conditions of human life to star-properties and elemental powers and then to invisible spirits, with a difficult “verschult”/sin and divine-forgiveness phrase. The conclusion includes God as giver/protector and the arts as help for body and soul. I preserve these claims rather than treating them as filler around a seasonal maxim.
