## Answer and distinction

**The owned sources do contain concrete, different text-to-picture roles in
complete pictured programs. None of the examined sources already establishes
the ownership of two f85 text positions.** This is not merely the general
observation that an external source cannot translate a manuscript. The
specific problem is that the closest sources assign different grammatical
owners to similar attributes:

- In Arundel 83 II f126v, the staff-user's inscription describes his own
  taking up a staff; the urinal scene's inscription describes the failing
  **patient**, while the physician is another actor in the same scene.
- The Laufenberg print distinguishes a short seasonal self-naming caption
  from a longer teaching poem. Its separate physician-associated regimen
  unit is instructional. A whole seasonal poem is not thereby the pictured
  season's uninterrupted speech.
- Egerton 2572 f51v has individual figure rubrics but a **collective
  definition distributed across their banners**. The neighboring figure is
  not automatically the grammatical subject of its banner portion.

These are useful alternative ownership models for the original composite
image hypothesis. They are more constraining than attaching unrelated
meteorological paragraphs to four portraits. However, source completeness,
source-internal ownership, resemblance to a target figure, and ownership of
a particular target passage are four separate claims. The last remains a
paid hypothesis here. No four-season assignment is required by this review.

## Concrete candidate consequence, and its strongest rival

**The sharpest available two-position candidate is an Arundel-like
life-episode account at South and East.** This is a prospective hypothesis,
not a new identification or a chosen writer. It does not first call any
position Spring, Summer, Autumn or Winter.

Its useful consequence would be an **asymmetric participant binding**:

1. At the support-bearing position, the person represented by the figure
   must also be the participant described as using support or suffering
   age-related weakness. A generic proposition about cold does not satisfy
   that obligation.
2. At the vessel position, a doctor-like actor and a person whose illness or
   decline is described must be separately introduced/related if the Arundel
   episode model is used. The source does not give the physician the
   patient's first-person state. A target proposal cannot silently alternate
   between those two people or invent a cure caused by the examination.
3. Any common grammatical reference construction must preserve those
   different bindings through the actual complete selected passages. It
   cannot mean “nearby figure” in one clause and “unpictured patient” in
   another without an overt, counted distinction. These are analytical
   obligations, not assignments to Voynich tokens or new parser rules.

This has a concrete potential failure: a fixed target account that forces
the same pictured owner to be both the medical examiner and the declining
patient, without a written relation or independently justified identity,
would fail this **specific** episode account. Conversely, a complete account
that explicitly distinguishes actor from described participant would satisfy
a substantive source-derived ownership requirement. It would remain an
exploratory fit. The manuscript does not have to inherit Arundel's ten scenes,
four outer summaries, divine centre, Latin language or reading direction.
Any such compression/omission would have to be chosen before authoring;
without that disclosure it would not be a complete source-equivalent reading.

The **strongest source-compatible rival** for that exact contrast is a
Laufenberg-like instructional arrangement: the vessel figure marks medical
teaching, and the text addresses a reader rather than reporting a pictured
patient's decline; a nearby aged figure supplies an analogy or exemplified
condition. Both traditions contain supports and a diagnostic vessel. Their
different participant ownership makes the rival serious. Egerton adds a
second structural rival: several figure-adjacent texts may continue one
collective explanation, with no individual autobiographical owner at all.
Md2 preserves the celestial-role rival for the vessel independently of
whether either medical-text model can be written.

The Laufenberg alternative also supplies a useful **West/East** contrast:
short figure self-description involving vegetation versus instructional
authority/addressee discourse. But its long seasonal bodies share teaching
language with the physician-associated unit. First-person/second-person
forms alone therefore do not separate those roles. Calling every West
paragraph a season speaking would repeat IDEA000538's unsupported transfer.

No confirmed word is required to propose either complete exploratory
account. Working meanings, introductions and role relations may be openly
paid assumptions. What would add decision value is the *written difference
between those ownership models across complete passages*, with every reused
form retained. Merely replacing a meteorological dictionary by an ageing
dictionary, or writing two independent owner labels that never constrain
anything downstream, would not do so.
